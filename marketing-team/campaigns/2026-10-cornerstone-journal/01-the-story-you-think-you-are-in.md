# The Story You Think You Are In

When people sit down across from me with a question, they rarely arrive with only a question. Usually, they arrive inside a story that has started to pinch at the shoulders.

It sounds like this: *I worked ten years to build this career, so why do I dread Monday mornings?* Or: *Everyone around me seems sure of their next five years, and I can barely see past November.* Or: *I thought I had finished grieving this change, and yet here it is again at the kitchen sink.*

Capable, thoughtful people are often very good editors of their own lives. We know how to keep a schedule, meet an obligation, and explain our choices in clear paragraphs when someone asks how things are going. The trouble starts when the chapter we are actually living no longer matches the outline we wrote five years ago.

When that happens, it is easy to assume that you have lost your footing. Often, though, you haven't lost your footing at all. You have simply outgrown the narrator you hired for an earlier season.

## Why We Bring Cards to a Conversation

There is a persistent rumor that tarot cards are for peeking around the corner of the calendar. People expect a verdict: take the job, sell the house, wait until spring.

I have never found predictions very useful. Even if someone could hand you a script for next year, it would rob you of the most important part of being alive, which is deciding how you want to show up in the room you are standing in today. Tarot is not about predicting the future. It is about illuminating the present.

When you turn over a card on a table, something simple and practical happens. The story that has been looping inside your head all week has to meet an image outside of you.

Suppose you have been telling yourself that you are failing because a project or a role has come to a halt. You feel behind. You feel like everyone else is moving at highway speed while you are parked on the shoulder. Then you turn over The Hanged Man, or the Four of Swords, or The Hermit.

Suddenly, you are looking at a picture of a deliberate pause. You are looking at a figure holding a single lantern that only lights the next two steps on the mountain, not the whole highway. And a quiet question enters the room: *What if this stillness isn't a mistake? What if it is the exact pace this decision requires?*

The card doesn't tell you what to do on Monday morning. Instead, it gives you a second angle on the facts. It lets you hear the difference between what happened and the harsh commentary you have been adding in the margins.

## Notice Where the Language Tightens

As a writer, I pay close attention to the words we use when we think we are merely stating facts.

In the middle of a transition, our vocabulary tends to shrink. Everything becomes *always*, *never*, *too late*, or *supposed to*. We talk about our lives as if we are characters trapped inside a genre we didn't choose: a courtroom drama where we have to defend every change of heart, or a race where pausing to tie a shoelace counts as forfeiting.

One of the kindest things you can do for yourself in an uncertain season is to put that story on paper where you can look at it in daylight.

When a thought stays in your head, it wears the authority of a voiceover. Once it is written down in black ink on an ordinary sheet of paper, it becomes a first draft. And the wonderful thing about a first draft is that you are allowed to cross a line out and try a truer sentence underneath.

## A Ten-Minute Prompt for an Unfinished Chapter

If you are carrying a decision or a transition that feels heavy this week, you do not need to figure out the ending tonight. Set aside ten minutes, pour a cup of tea or light a candle beside your notebook, and try this three-step exercise on a clean page.

### Step 1: Write the Camera Version (3 Minutes)
Describe the situation you are in using only plain, observable facts, the way a camera in the corner of the room would record it. Leave out adjectives like *impossible*, *overdue*, or *obvious*. Write down what changed, what was said, and what question is currently sitting on the table.

### Step 2: Name the Genre You've Been Living In (3 Minutes)
Read those plain facts back to yourself, and then finish this sentence three or four times:
*The story I have been telling myself about this is...*

Notice what tone that story takes. Is it rushing you? Is it comparing you to an older version of yourself? Is it demanding that you know step ten before you have taken step one?

### Step 3: Ask for One Quiet Alternative (4 Minutes)
If you have a tarot deck nearby, shuffle and pull a single card. Don't reach for a guidebook right away. Look at the posture of the figure on the card, the weather in the background, or the objects on the table. Then finish this sentence on your page:
*If I didn't have to rush this chapter, another way to read this moment might be...*

If you don't use cards, simply ask the page: *What is one thing that is still true and steady here, even while the rest is unfinished?*
