# Keeping Company With a Question

Most of us were trained to treat an unanswered question like an untidy desk. The goal is to clear it off as quickly as possible so we can get back to being productive.

In our work lives, that habit serves us well. You see a problem, you gather the data, you make a call before five o'clock, and you move on to the next item on the list.

The trouble is that the big questions of a human life do not behave like inbox items.

*How do I want to spend the next decade of my work?*
*What does a healthy boundary look like with people I care about?*
*Who am I becoming now that an old role has ended?*

When you try to force a question like that to give up its answer on a Tuesday afternoon between meetings, you usually end up with a thin answer. You pick the option that sounds easiest to explain at a dinner party, or you fall back on a rule you inherited twenty years ago without checking whether it still fits the person you are today.

There is another way to treat a large question. Instead of interrogating it under a bright lamp until it confesses, you can learn to keep company with it.

## The Space Between the Cards

In tarot, we talk a lot about the images on the cards, yet some of the most honest moments in a reading happen in the quiet seconds between them.

When someone brings a complex question to a session, say, whether to step away from a long-held role or how to navigate a season of change, the first five minutes are often full of explanations. We lay out the pros and cons we have already rehearsed a hundred times.

Then we lay down a spread. We look at Temperance pouring water slowly between two cups, or The Empress seated in a garden where things grow at the speed of seasons, not at the speed of willpower.

And for a moment, the rehearsed speeches stop. There is a pause where neither of us is trying to fix the situation. We are simply looking at it together, noticing where the weight is sitting, and asking what the question is actually asking of you.

Keeping company with a question means allowing it to stay open while you live alongside it for a little while. It means trusting that you do not have to decide everything today in order to be paying attention today.

## Why Physical Rituals Help the Mind Slow Down

When you spend most of your day looking at screens and answering other people's priorities, switching gears into reflective thinking is harder than it sounds. You sit down with a journal, and for the first ten minutes your mind is still drafting replies to colleagues or reminding you to buy groceries.

This is why small, repeatable physical cues matter so much to writers and contemplative practitioners.

You do not need an elaborate altar or an hour of silence. You only need one or two sensory markers that tell your nervous system and your attention: *This twenty-minute window belongs to my own thoughts.*

When we created the [Intention Candles in the shop](/shop), particularly *The Writing Hour* and *Protection*, this was the exact moment we had in mind. A candle does not do your thinking for you. What it offers is a boundary in time. You strike a match, watch the flame settle beside your notebook, and know that while that small light is burning, nobody needs a finished answer from you.

## A Twenty-Minute Writing Hour Ritual

If you are living with a question that refuses to be solved in a hurry, try giving it a dedicated twenty-minute appointment once a week rather than letting it follow you around all day in the back of your mind.

Here is a simple structure you can use at your kitchen table or desk tonight:

### 1. Mark the Beginning (2 Minutes)
Clear a small space the size of an open notebook. Put your phone in another room. Light a candle, pour a glass of water or tea, and sit for sixty seconds without picking up your pen. Let the day catch up with you.

### 2. Write the Question in Plain Ink (3 Minutes)
At the top of the page, write the question you are carrying. Then rewrite it two more times directly underneath, making it slightly simpler and more honest each time. Often, the first question we write is the polite version (*"Should I take on this extra responsibility?"*), and the third version is the real one (*"Why do I feel responsible for holding everything together?"*).

### 3. Invite One Image to the Table (5 Minutes)
If you work with tarot, pull one card and set it beside your notebook. Describe what the figure on the card is doing with their hands, where they are looking, and what stands behind them. Ask: *Where in my question does this posture feel familiar?*

### 4. Write Without Crossing Out (8 Minutes)
Keep your pen moving for eight minutes. Start with the stem: *What I don't know yet is...* followed a few lines later by: *What I do know for sure today is...* Let both lists exist on the same page without forcing them to agree.

### 5. Close the Page on Purpose (2 Minutes)
Read back what you wrote and underline one single sentence that feels honest. Close the notebook, blow out the candle, and leave the question on the desk until your next writing hour.
